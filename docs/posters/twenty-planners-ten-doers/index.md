Use **Explore** mode to hover over a numbered marker or its label and read what that part of the team does. Use **Quiz** mode to read a clue and click the marker that matches. The marker numbers are hidden in Quiz mode, so you must find the matching part of the stage. Planners are on the top tier, the task belt is in the middle, and the doers are at the bottom.

The twelve callouts are:

1. **Task Queue** — The ordered list of waiting tasks, kept as a plain file everyone reads.
2. **Handoff Card** — The goal, inputs, constraints, criteria, and status that pass from one agent to the next.
3. **Critical Path** — The longest chain of dependent tasks, which extra agents cannot shorten.
4. **Planning Agent Bank** — About twenty agents that explore, decompose, and specify.
5. **Review Checkpoint** — A point where output is examined before the next step uses it, often automated.
6. **Human Approval Gate** — A checkpoint where a person must say yes.
7. **Execution Agent Bank** — About ten agents that carry out specified tasks.
8. **The Orchestrator** — The researcher, who coordinates the work and stays accountable.
9. **Shared Workspace** — The common area for files, with separate outputs and reviewed merges.
10. **Status Board** — The morning report: finished, blocked, needs a decision, and spent.
11. **Conflict Flag** — Two agents with incompatible results, traced to the first point of divergence.
12. **Attention Meter** — The review budget that sets how big a team can usefully be.

## Why This Matters

Chapter 10 describes an agent team as planning agents plus execution agents with explicit handoffs. Planning output is cheap and reversible, so many plans can run in parallel. Execution output changes files and costs more attention to check, so that side stays smaller. The chapter's arithmetic makes the point: 20 planners, 10 executors, and 30 minutes of conflict resolution need 300 minutes of review, which is more than a 4-hour budget unless you sample, automate checks, or shrink the team.

The numbers on the poster are the chapter's illustrative figures. The 20-to-10 ratio is a design target, not a law, and the right team size depends on how checkable your tasks are and how much attention you have. The poster does not claim that more agents get more done. Dependencies, review cost, and coordination all cap the benefit.
